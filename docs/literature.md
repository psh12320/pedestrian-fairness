# Research positioning: pedestrian-aware signal control

This is a selective reading of seven primary studies, not a claim that the wider literature has been exhausted. The papers establish that pedestrian-aware reinforcement learning (RL), multi-junction control, real-map simulation, and vehicle–pedestrian reward comparisons already exist. Team 38's defensible contribution is a carefully controlled test of **who waits**, especially at individual crossings and at the end of a finite simulation. Results should be described as simulated outcomes, not as evidence of better deployed signals.

## What prior work actually tests

| Study | What it tests | Relevance and boundary for Team 38 |
|---|---|---|
| IVPL [1] | Deep RL for joint vehicle and pedestrian signal timing at a Melbourne intersection. Its microsimulation uses observed camera and loop-detector data and real SCATS signal parameters; it compares against actuated control and examines jaywalking. | Joint road-user delay is established. Our distinct measurement question is whether average gains hide long waits at particular protected crossings. |
| Pedestrian-queue max-pressure [2] | A decentralized, non-RL pressure controller using vehicle and pedestrian queues, compared in SUMO with vehicle-only and rule-based pedestrian pressure methods under several vehicle loads. | A strong pedestrian-aware conventional baseline. A simplified implementation in our environment must use our common safety state machine and be labelled an adaptation, not a reproduction of their result or stability proof. |
| Poudel et al. [3] | A **single-agent** PPO controller for eight signals along a real corridor, using pedestrian and vehicle demand derived from Wi-Fi logs and video. It tests demand outside the training range and reports mean waits. | Real-demand virtual testing is feasible. Our comparison of local IPPO/MAPPO actors is different, but an average-wait improvement alone is not a new research question. |
| Helsinki pedestrian-aware MAPPO [4] | Curiosity-enhanced MAPPO on a real-map-based, 16-signal Helsinki SUMO network. It compares vehicle-only and pedestrian-aware rewards, multiple demand levels, and several RL baselines. Its reported pedestrian metrics include network-average wait and average number waiting. | The reward trade-off and multi-intersection pedestrian-aware MAPPO are prior work. The paper's listed metrics do not include per-crossing p95 or requests pending at the horizon; those are useful focused measurements for us, without claiming nobody has measured them elsewhere. |
| PedSLight [5] | Safety-aware PPO with a time-to-collision surrogate at one intersection and a three-intersection corridor, compared with fixed/actuated baselines under varying demand. | A three-junction pedestrian-aware PPO simulation is not novel by itself. Our protected phases address signal conflicts, but do not measure all real pedestrian conflict risks or prove crash reduction. |
| RESCO [6] | A traffic-signal RL benchmark with realistic scenarios and multiple methods; its experiments show that rankings can change with sensing assumptions and less stylized layouts. | Motive for a held-out network, turning movements, demand variation, and imperfect detection, rather than relying on one synthetic straight-through corridor. |
| MARL evaluation protocol [7] | A review of 75 cooperative MARL papers and an evaluation protocol advocating independent training runs, uncertainty estimates, complete experimental details, and ablations. | Five demand seeds for one trained model do not establish learning stability. Repeat training with independent initializations and report training curves and compute. |

## Questions to test in this project

The final protocol has six learned method–reward configurations, three conventional controllers, independent training replicas, held-out demand seeds, event-defined pedestrian waits, and common legal phases. Its linear policy class and synthetic demand remain limitations. Earlier exploratory results had few crossing-level entries and little congestion; these motivated the stronger locked tests, not a claim that an algorithm had already won.

1. **Trade-off and concentration.** At matched demand, does increasing the pedestrian reward weight change vehicle time loss, per-crossing mean and p95 wait, and unserved requests? Plot the trade-off across prespecified weights. A lower network mean with a worse crossing-level tail is an important negative finding.
2. **Coordination.** Does a network-wide training critic help MAPPO relative to IPPO when short links create inter-junction spillback? Compare them with the same actor observations, reward, phase rules, training budget, and demand seeds. Equal performance would indicate no demonstrated coordination gain under these conditions.
3. **Service under overload.** At what demand do any controllers leave vehicles uninserted or unfinished, or pedestrians without a crossing entry? Vehicle time loss for completed trips alone can improve while total service deteriorates.
4. **Robustness and external validity.** How do results change with slower walkers, burst arrivals, imperfect pedestrian detection, and a held-out irregular or Singapore-derived map? A real map is only a geometry test until demand and timings are supported by observed data and calibration checks.

For all comparisons, count crossing entries and pending requests; avoid interpreting a p95 based on a handful of entries as stable. Use paired demand seeds, multiple independent training seeds, uncertainty intervals, and the same signal-safety wrapper for every controller. Include a pedestrian-aware pressure baseline [2]. Keep the present result if RL loses to it or if IPPO and MAPPO remain indistinguishable; explain *when* the simpler method is sufficient.
